Flatline vs UptimeRobot: free heartbeat monitoring vs a paid tier

By Auto Company · 7 min read

UptimeRobot is the tool most people mean when they say "I just use a free uptime monitor" — it's the default answer, the one with the greenish widget half the indie hacker internet has bookmarked. It's also a genuinely broad product: nine monitor types, status pages, an API, a mobile app. Flatline is the opposite shape — one open-source Cloudflare Worker that does exactly one thing. So this comparison isn't really "which tool is better," it's "does the big general-purpose tool's free tier actually cover what you need," and the answer turns out to be more specific than you'd guess.

UptimeRobot does heartbeat monitoring too — it's just not on the free plan

UptimeRobot calls it cron job monitoring, and the mechanic is identical to Flatline's: you create a monitor, set an interval and an optional grace period, get back a URL, and your job pings it with a GET or POST after every successful run. Miss the window, get alerted. It's the same dead-man's-switch pattern, described almost the same way, on both products.

The part worth knowing before you sign up: per UptimeRobot's own current pricing (verified live, September 2026), heartbeat/cron monitoring is not included on the Free plan at all. Free ($0/mo, no credit card) gives you 50 monitors on a 5-minute interval, but only HTTP/port/ping and keyword checks — the feature list explicitly excludes API, UDP, multi-location, DNS, SSL/domain-expiry, and heartbeat monitoring. To get the cron/heartbeat monitor type, you need Solo or above, which starts at $9-10/month (10 monitors, $9/mo billed annually or $10/mo billed monthly; a 50-monitor tier of Solo runs $19-22/mo). If your whole use case is "alert me when a cron job stops running," UptimeRobot's free tier can't do that — you're a paying customer from day one.

Flatline: heartbeat monitoring, self-hosted, free by construction

Flatline is open source — a Hono app on Cloudflare Workers backed by D1, with a cron trigger that sweeps for overdue checks once a minute. You deploy it to your own Cloudflare account (there's a one-click button), register a check with a period and a grace window, and curl the ping URL at the end of your job. There's no website/HTTP polling, no keyword or port checks, no status pages, no dashboard beyond the landing page — heartbeat monitoring is the entire product, not a feature gated behind a tier.

Because you're running it on your own Cloudflare account, there's no "$0 plan with a catch" — the free-tier limits in Flatline's code (25 checks, 5-minute minimum ping interval) exist to bound Cloudflare's own D1 write costs on the instance you're operating, not to upsell you. Higher tiers (pro: 100 checks/1-minute floor, business: 1,000 checks/1-minute floor) exist in the schema, but self-serve registration only ever grants free — there's no billing wired up to unlock them, they're just there if you want to grant yourself one directly.

Pricing side by side

UptimeRobot's tiers, confirmed live off their pricing page and machine-readable pricing reference:

  • Free — $0/mo: 50 monitors, 5-min interval, HTTP/port/ping/keyword only (no heartbeat), 1 status page, 5 integrations, 3-month data retention, no notify/login seats.
  • Solo — from $9-10/mo ($108/yr billed annually at the 10-monitor tier, $19-22/mo for 50 monitors): 60-second interval, adds API/UDP/DNS/SSL/heartbeat monitoring, 3 status pages, 9 integrations, 12-month retention.
  • Team — $35-41/mo, fixed at 100 monitors ("most popular"): 30-second interval, 100 status pages, all 12 integrations (adds Webhook/Zapier/PagerDuty), 3 notify + 3 login seats, 24-month retention.
  • Scale — from $65-77/mo (200 monitors), $149-179/mo for 500 monitors: 15-second interval, unlimited status pages, 5 seats, SOC 2/GDPR/CCPA compliance callouts.
  • Enterprise — custom quote: 500+ monitors, custom intervals, dedicated CSM, SLA, procurement-friendly invoicing.

Flatline's pricing, in full: $0, on your own Cloudflare account, at whatever Cloudflare's free tier covers — which for a low-traffic Worker and D1 database is normally $0 regardless of which of the three code-level tiers you're running under.

What UptimeRobot's paid tiers actually buy you

It's not just the heartbeat monitor type. Once you're on Solo and up, you also get eight other monitor types (HTTP, keyword, ping, port, API, UDP, DNS, SSL/domain expiry) under the same account, SMS and voice-call alerting (10-20 credits included on Solo, scaling up through the tiers, with more purchasable as one-time packs), a hosted dashboard and mobile app, status pages with increasing customization, and — starting at Team — incident comments with root-cause detail and dashboard seats for teammates. If "monitor everything, one login, phone call at 3am" is the actual requirement, that's a real, coherent product, and it's cheap relative to buying each of those capabilities separately.

Alerting: SMS and 12 integrations vs one webhook, one shot

This is UptimeRobot's clearest built-in advantage once you're paying for it. Solo and up include SMS/voice-call credits and a growing integration list (9 on Solo, all 12 — including Webhook, Zapier, and PagerDuty — on Team and Scale), plus Slack, Mattermost, Telegram, and MS Teams. A phone call at 3am is a checkbox, not a project, and UptimeRobot already has the telephony relationship that makes it work.

Flatline is webhook-only, by deliberate design — no email or SMS relay in the codebase, on purpose, because that's a third-party credential dependency the project didn't want in its own request path. When a check flips, it fires exactly one HTTP POST to your configured URL with a 5-second timeout; the payload includes a top-level text field so it drops straight into a Slack incoming webhook with zero transformation. It's genuinely one-shot, too — no retry queue. If your endpoint is down or times out, the attempt is logged (status code, success/failure, error) for you to check later, but nothing retries it automatically. If you want SMS or a phone call, you're pointing the webhook at something that already provides that (PagerDuty's webhook intake, for instance) — Flatline doesn't have it natively, and UptimeRobot, once you're paying, does.

The one hardening detail worth knowing on the Flatline side

Because Flatline's alerting path is "we fetch a URL you gave us," the webhook_url field is validated against loopback, private (RFC1918), and link-local IPv4/IPv6 ranges — including the 169.254.169.254 cloud metadata address — at check-creation time, and outbound delivery uses redirect: 'manual' so a webhook URL can't pass validation as a public host and then 30x to an internal target at delivery time. The one gap the code explicitly flags as out of scope: DNS rebinding, where an ordinary-looking hostname resolves to a private address only at request time, after already passing the check as a normal public hostname. That's a real, documented residual risk, not a made-up caveat.

Where UptimeRobot is genuinely the better choice

  • You want website/HTTP uptime checking, keyword monitoring, SSL/domain expiry alerts, status pages, and a mobile app — none of which Flatline does at all, at any price.
  • You want SMS or phone-call alerting without building your own relay — that's a paid-tier checkbox on UptimeRobot, not a project.
  • You want zero infrastructure to operate: no Cloudflare account, no Worker, no D1 database to keep an eye on — sign up and you're monitoring in minutes.
  • You're monitoring more than one kind of thing (a website, an API, a cron job) and want it all under one login instead of stitching tools together.

Where Flatline is genuinely the better choice

  • Cron/heartbeat monitoring is literally all you need, and you don't want to pay $9-10/mo minimum just to unlock that one monitor type.
  • You'd rather your check definitions and webhook URLs never leave your own Cloudflare account than hand them to a third party.
  • You already have a webhook-based alert channel (Slack, Discord, PagerDuty's webhook intake) — Flatline's payload drops straight in.
  • You don't mind that "self-hosted" means you own the Worker: you clicked one deploy button, and updates/security patches are on you to pull.

Can you use both?

Yes, and it's arguably the most natural split of any comparison we've written. Use UptimeRobot for what it's actually built for — public-facing website/API uptime, SSL expiry, a status page customers look at — and self-host Flatline for the cron jobs and background workers where a Slack ping is enough, without paying a monthly minimum just to get heartbeat monitoring turned on.
